Mission Control

Mission Control is the home screen. It shows the whole system on one page: five operators train one model together without sharing data, the model is tested and rolled out, a governed team of AI agents runs a simulated network, and every step lands in a signed ledger. Each panel summarises a deeper screen; the five plane tiles, the twin map and the ribbon are links to those screens.

Route / Go to it with g then m Appears in demo beats 1 14

The screen at a glance

Mission Control belongs to no single plane. It is the overview that sits above all five, which is why its header carries all three track tags (T1, T2, T3). Think of it as the system's one-slide summary: if a judge only ever saw this page, they should still understand what SPECTRUM does.

The one-sentence story

"Five operators train one network model together without sharing data, the model has to pass a test before it ships, a team of agents uses it to run the network under policy and human approval, and every step is signed so anyone can check it later."

The page is built from five blocks, read top to bottom:

  • Network health (top left): is the network OK right now? This is the Operate plane's headline.
  • Consortium (top right): the five operators training together around a sealed aggregator. This is the Learn plane.
  • Five plane tiles (middle row): three numbers and a small trend line for each of Learn, Verify, Release, Operate and Prove.
  • Network twin (bottom left): a small map of the 40 simulated cell sites.
  • Needs attention (bottom right): the last eight warnings or critical events from anywhere in the system.

Around the page you also see the console frame: the top bar, the five-colour lifecycle ribbon, the screens rail on the left and the live stream line at the bottom. They are the same on every screen and are explained on Console frame & controls.

Using it as the one-slide summary

When a judge asks "so what is this?", stay on this page and walk the five blocks in this order. Each step is one sentence and one gesture.

  1. Ribbon: sweep left to right. "Learn, verify, release, operate, prove: the life of one model."
  2. Consortium: "Five operators train together. Only masked updates reach the aggregator, which in AWS mode sits in a sealed Nitro Enclave, and zero bytes of raw data cross a boundary."
  3. Tiles: touch each one. "Training round and privacy spend; the model in production and the one being tested; what is serving; the agents; the signed ledger."
  4. Network health and twin: "This is the network the agents run: 40 sites in a simulated city with a stadium district."
  5. Needs attention: "Anything that needs a human surfaces here, and every row opens its signed record."

Tiles, the twin map and the ribbon are doors

Each tile, the twin map and the ribbon are links. If a judge asks a deep question about one plane, click its tile and answer on that screen, then press g m to come back. Network health and Consortium are not links; to go deeper use the Operate or Learn tile. Needs attention rows open the Inspector, not another screen.

60-second walkthrough

Use this if a judge says "walk me through this page". About 150 words, spoken slowly.

How it works

Changes when

The twin is a small simulated city: 40 sites on an 8 by 5 grid, three cells each, fed by five aggregation hubs. For every site it works out demand (a base level that drifts slightly, a slow daily wave, plus any surge), capacity (how many cells are awake, cut to 2% if the fibre is down, to 90% on microwave backup), and from those the PRB load. PRB (physical resource block) load is the share of the radio capacity in use. Users are demand × 1,400 per site; throughput is the served demand × 820 Mbps per site, summed and shown in Gbps.

When beat 7 cuts fibre T3, every site on hub H3 (the 10 sites in the two middle columns, including the stadium district) loses backhaul, so health falls to 75% or lower and the pill turns Impaired. When the agents reroute those sites over microwave, they count as OK again unless the stadium surge pushes their load past 85%.

How to read it

Green pill and a number near 100% is normal. A sudden drop with a red pill means sites have lost backhaul or are overloaded: an incident is starting.

How it works

Backend

Arrives by

  1. Pull1 s
  2. Trainabout 16 s, 240 steps
  3. Secure1.4 s, masking

  5. Aggregate2.6 s, in enclave

A round in plain words. Each operator downloads the current shared adapter (a LoRA adapter: a small add-on to the base model that is the only part being trained), trains it on its own data with DP-SGD, masks the result and uploads it. The masks are random numbers that cancel exactly when all updates are added up, so outside the enclave nobody can read a single operator's update. Inside the enclave, the aggregator compares the size (norm, meaning the update's magnitude, not its byte size) of each update with the others and rejects any far above the median, such as a poisoned update, then produces the new shared model. This robust aggregation is configured as "trimmed mean" and only works in enclave mode, where the aggregator can see individual updates.

How ε is accounted. ε (epsilon) measures how much a model could reveal about any one training record; lower is more private. DP-SGD clips each example's gradient and adds noise. In this engine every training step adds to the operator's squared epsilon: Δ(ε²) = (1.71 × q / σ)² × steps × ln(1/δ), where q = 64 ÷ the operator's dataset size, σ = 1.1 (noise) and δ = 10⁻⁵. Small datasets mean a bigger q, so they spend faster. That is why Op-E, with 7,000 examples, is highest (ε 2.59) and Op-D, with 22,600, is lowest (ε 0.80).

The budget rule. At 90% of the target ε of 3.0 the engine emits a warning (fl.budget, which also shows in Needs attention). At 3.0 the operator's status becomes budget_exhausted, it leaves the federation and its circle fades. In the beat 14 shot Op-E sits at ε 3.00 with 346.0 MiB (16 uploads) while the others reached 389.3 MiB (18 uploads).

The squares are computed in the browser: the panel watches new fl.upload and fl.round.phase (phase pull) events in the stream and animates each one for 1.3 seconds.

The percentage is computed in the browser from the last round: take the lowest of the five "local only" losses (what each operator would get training alone), then (best alone − federated) ÷ best alone. At round 12 the best alone is Op-D at about 1.11, and the federated loss is 0.704, so the gain is about 36%.

Both losses come from formulas in the engine. The federated loss follows 0.56 + 1.72 × e−0.21 × round plus small noise. Each operator's local-only loss follows its own floor + 1.5 × e−0.16 × round. So the federated curve falls faster and lower, which is the expected benefit of pooling knowledge.

The "0 B" figure is written directly in the page code. The engine also keeps a raw-bytes counter that stays at zero, which the Federation Center shows in its data-sovereignty view.

How it works

Backend

Arrives by
Snapshot, then ledger.append, ledger.anchor, ledger.verify
Store slice
ledger.height, ledger.last_verify, ledger.anchors, trust.series
Changes when
Every signed action adds a record; beat 12 tampers with a record and the Audit screen runs a check 1.2 s later
  1. Action happensapproval, tool call, round, stage…
  2. HashSHA-256 of the record + previous hash
  3. SignEd25519 with the actor's key
  4. Anchorevery 32 records, Merkle root

Each record's hash includes the previous record's hash, so changing any old record breaks every link after it. Every 32 records the engine builds a Merkle tree over their hashes (a tree of hashes whose single root summarises all 32) and writes the root as an anchor. In AWS mode the anchor goes to an S3 bucket with Object Lock in compliance mode, which means nobody can delete or overwrite it during the retention period; in mock mode it is kept in memory with the same fields.

The check (POST /api/ledger/verify) walks the whole chain and stops at the first record whose hash, link or signature does not match. Beat 12 edits one anchored approval record directly in the datastore, and the Audit screen runs the check, which reports "broken at #3503". The tile only reads the last result; it never runs the check itself.
