SPECTRUM in 60 seconds

SPECTRUM is our entry for Breaking Barriers at FYUZ 2026 (TIP and AWS). The theme is network large models (NLMs): AI models trained for telecom network operations. The console you will demo shows one such model's whole life, from training to proof, on one screen.

The problem

Every operator has outages. Each one learns from its own outages alone, because no operator can share subscriber or incident data with a competitor.

  • A model trained on one operator's data only knows that operator's vendors and faults.
  • AI agents that change a live network are risky: who checks what they do, and who can prove it later?

The idea

Five operators fine-tune one shared model without moving their data. The model is tested before release, rolled out carefully, and then powers a swarm of agents that fix outages under rules they cannot get around. Every step is signed into a tamper-evident record.

  • Only masked model updates leave each operator. Raw incident data stays home.
  • Policy is checked by code outside the AI model, and big changes wait for a human.

Why it matters to the judges

The hackathon has three tracks. Most console screens show small track tags next to their title, such as T1, T2 and T3, so judges can see which track a screen covers. Some screens also show Trust or Obs: these are cross-cutting themes, not tracks.

How the console works, in one path

Almost every number you see follows the same path. Once you know it, every screen makes sense.

  1. Mock enginePython simulation in backend/spectrum/, ticks 10 times a second
  2. Eventseach engine emits typed events such as fl.round, eval.gate, ledger.append
  3. One WebSocket/ws sends events in batches every 100 ms
  4. Browser storeone slice per engine (federation, swarm, ledger…)
  5. Panels re-rendereach panel reads the slices it needs
  • On connect, the browser loads the full current state from GET /api/snapshot, then applies newer events from the WebSocket. If the connection drops it reconnects and loads the snapshot again.
  • Commands go the other way as REST calls, for example POST /api/director/beat/7 or POST /api/ledger/verify. The engine acts, emits events, and every open console updates.
  • Demo beats live on the server. Firing a beat moves the browser to the beat's screen, lights its panels (if spotlight is on), and posts the beat. The server runs the scenario step and broadcasts a director.beat event, so a second laptop and the projector stay in step.
  • Spotlight is what makes most of the screen fade after a beat. Every panel except the ones to talk about drops to about a third of its brightness, and the lit panels get a coloured outline. The ribbon never fades. Press Esc to clear it, or s to turn spotlight off.
  • The starting state is pre-warmed. On start or reset the engine fast-forwards a fixed-seed history: 12 training rounds done and about 3,400 signed ledger records. So every run starts from the same point.

What is real and what is simulated

The console runs on a mock engine. It says so: the top bar shows MOCK, and each panel has a small source chip naming the AWS service it maps to.

  • Real The ledger (SHA-256 hash chain, Ed25519 signatures, Merkle anchors), secure aggregation (masks cancel with zero error), signed agent-to-agent messages, and policy checks run outside the model by a deterministic evaluator that mirrors the Cedar policies shown (Cedar is an AWS language for writing access rules).
  • Simulated Training curves and the privacy accountant, the network twin's KPIs, model benchmark scores, and token prices.
  • The AWS adapters in backend/spectrum/adapters/aws.py are thin stubs with real boto3 call shapes, not yet tested against a live account. Do not claim a live AWS deployment.

panel ribbon, file app/Ribbon.tsx

What it is for

One line that tells you where each plane stands right now, and the fastest way to jump between planes.

What it shows

Five coloured segments: Learn, Verify, Release, Operate, Prove. Each has a main reading and a smaller reading on the right. In the screenshot: "Round 13 queued" with "ε max 2.59 of 3", "nlm-v6 in prod", "nlm-v6 serving" with "4 targets, 2 vendors", "46 agents, 0 incidents" with "network nominal", and "Ledger #3,434" with "anchored 49 s ago" (time since the last Merkle anchor was written). The right-hand reading turns red when a plane needs attention, for example "chain broken at #N".

ε (epsilon) is how much privacy an operator has spent; lower is more private. "ε max 2.59 of 3" is the highest spend of any operator against the budget of 3. An operator that reaches its budget leaves the federation.

Which round are we on? 12 rounds are finished and round 13 is next. The Learn tile counts finished rounds ("12 of 30"), and the ribbon names the next one ("Round 13 queued"). Beat 2 starts round 13, and the ribbon then reads "Round 13/30".

Each segment reads the latest state of its plane from the live feed and picks a short text. Nothing is fetched on demand.

Consortium

panel consortium

What it is for

Shows the five operators training one model together, and that only masked model updates travel between them. This is the cold-open picture (beat 1).

What it shows

  • Five operator circles (Op-A to Op-E) around a centre box, "Enclave aggregator". Each circle shows its ε (privacy spent so far; lower is more private) and its count of private incidents, for example "22,600 private incidents".
  • Each line is labelled with the adapter bytes that operator has uploaded (259.5 MiB each here). The adapter is the small set of extra weights each operator trains (LoRA); only this, masked, ever leaves the operator.
  • The centre shows the round state ("round 13 queued", or the current phase) and "PCR0 attested" when the aggregator runs in enclave mode. An enclave is a sealed AWS Nitro compute box that even its operators cannot look inside; PCR0 is the fingerprint of the code running in it, and "attested" means that fingerprint was checked.
  • Bottom row: "Raw data moved across boundaries 0 B", "Masked adapter updates moved" (1.3 GiB) and "Federated loss vs best alone" (0.704, 36% lower than the best operator training alone).

How it works

The panel draws the federation's latest state from the live feed and animates each upload as it happens.

Backend
engines/federation.py
Store slice
federation (operators, sovereignty, rounds log)
Changes when
A round runs: small packets fly to the centre on each fl.upload event and back out when a round's "pull" phase starts.

The loss comparison uses the last round's federated validation loss against the lowest local-only loss. Full card: Mission Control; the training itself is on Federation Center.

How to run it

Full instructions, including Windows and troubleshooting, are in SETUP_AND_RUN_instructions.md. You need Node 20.19 or newer and uv; no AWS account or internet is needed after install.

  1. In the project folder, run make install once.
  2. Run make dev. This starts the API on port 8787 and the console on port 5173. Open http://localhost:5173 in Chrome.
  3. On demo day, run make demo instead. It builds the console and serves everything from one process at http://localhost:8787, with no dev server.

Before you go on stage

  1. Find the Demo Director: a floating panel near the bottom-left of the console, titled "Demo Director" with "beat 0 of 14" next to it. It is open by default. If you cannot see it, press d. You can drag it by its title bar.
  2. Hold the Reset button in the Director for 1.2 s (or hold r for 1 s). Wait for the toast "Scenario reset to the pre-demo state". The console returns to Mission Control and the Learn segment of the ribbon reads "Round 13 queued". Do this even on a freshly started server: a fresh make dev is already training, starting with round 13, and only a reset holds it at "queued" so beat 2 can start it on cue.
  3. Click the Preflight tab in the Director and wait up to 10 s for the rows to refresh (they reload every 10 s). Check the rows: Backend reachable, WebSocket live, Ledger verified clean, Scenario reset (beat 0), Fonts loaded, Theme chosen, Approval phones connected. All should show a green tick. "Ledger verified clean" stays amber after a tamper rehearsal (beat 12) until you reset or restore the ledger on Audit.
  4. On a washed-out projector, press t for the light Projector theme.
  5. Phones: join the same Wi-Fi as the laptop and scan the QR code on the Preflight tab. It opens /m/approve. The "Approval phones connected" row counts every other open console, including the presenter laptop or a second browser tab, so check the phone itself shows its "Signing as" line. If you are not using phones, this row can stay amber; approve from the Oversight Desk instead.
  6. Spotlight and Notes are on by default (toggles at the bottom of the Director). Leave them on.

Single letters do things

Outside a text box, p pauses the simulation, t switches theme and s switches spotlight. Digit keys 1 to 9 fire that beat at once, even out of order: a stray 7 starts the incident. The go-to keys use the same letters only after g. Holding r resets everything, so never rest a finger on it. The full key map is on Console frame and controls.

What you seeWhat to do
Top bar says "Offline" or "Lagging"Check the terminal running make dev or make demo. When the server is back, the page reconnects and reloads the full state by itself.

Two-minute elevator pitch

About 350 words, which is a little over two minutes spoken at a calm pace. Every claim here is backed by something on screen. Pause where the paragraphs break.

"Every mobile operator has outages, and every operator learns from them alone, because nobody can share subscriber data with a competitor. SPECTRUM changes that. It is one console for the whole life of a network model, in five steps."

"Learn. Five operators fine-tune one shared model together. Each trains on its own private incidents, with privacy noise added, and each has a hard privacy budget. Only masked updates leave an operator. The masks cancel only in the total, so from any one operator our server sees only noise. If one operator is hacked and sends a bad update, the aggregator throws it out, inside a sealed, verified hardware box."

"Verify. The new model has to earn its place: accuracy, a safety red team, speed, price, and private scores from each operator. It gets a signed parts list, and a deploy gate decides."

"Release. One signed manifest ships it to four targets across two vendors. Shadow first, then ten percent of traffic, watched by safety limits on speed and errors."

"Operate. The model powers a swarm of 46 agents running a simulated network of 40 sites. When a fibre is cut after a concert, the agents negotiate, energy against customer experience, and the twin predicts the result before anything touches the network. The plan touches more than our five percent limit, so a human approves it, from a phone. When someone plants an instruction in a log line, the fooled agent is blocked by policy that runs outside the model, its messages are rejected, and it is quarantined."

"Prove. Every step is signed and chained into a ledger, each record linked to the one before. Edit one record and verification breaks at exactly that record. One click traces any action back to the approver, the model and the training round that produced it."

"And tonight's incident becomes tomorrow's private training data. That is the loop."

"To be straight with you: this runs on a mock engine. The ledger, secure aggregation, signed agent messages and policy checks are real code. Training curves, network numbers, benchmark scores and prices are simulated, and every panel says which AWS service it maps to."
